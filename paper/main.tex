\title{Neural Theorem Proving on miniF2F: \\
Scaling, Tree Search, and the Limits of Fine-Tuning}

\author{Aslan Feng}

\date{Columbia Business School REU, Summer 2026 \\
Mentor: Professor Namkoong}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
We investigate how far a single off-the-shelf theorem-proving model can be pushed on
the miniF2F benchmark under realistic compute constraints: one A40 GPU, 4-bit
quantization, and a fixed per-problem time budget.
Starting from DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL with whole-proof generation,
we document a series of experiments whose failures are as informative as their successes.
Whole-proof sampling reaches 39.8\% pass@1 at 64 samples per problem, then plateaus
completely: going to 256 samples yields zero additional verified proofs.
Adding a BFS tactic search (depth at most 6, width 8) improves the score to 48.8\%,
but the mechanism is more nuanced than it first appears: of the 25
problems uniquely solved by the tactic search configuration, only 8 are attributable to tree
structure itself; the remaining 17 come from the larger whole-proof fallback budget
(400 vs.\ 256 samples); and 3 problems solved by $k=256$ are not solved by tactic search,
giving a net gain of $25 - 3 = 22$ problems.
Two rounds of LoRA fine-tuning both degraded performance.
We trace this failure to a distributional mismatch: 98\% of the available
fine-tuning proofs are single-line tactic closers, while 63\% of the problems
successfully solved on miniF2F require multi-step proofs.
Fine-tuning amplified exactly the capability the benchmark does not need.
The remaining gap to the published 60.2\% reflects a 50\% hard tail of competition
problems that require proof structures neither strategy can construct, not a
gap that more samples would close.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

Automated theorem proving has long been a benchmark for machine reasoning.
The introduction of large language models trained specifically on formal
mathematics---in particular, on Lean 4 and its Mathlib library---has led
to dramatic improvements on standard benchmarks.
DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL \cite{deepseek} achieves 60.2\% on the miniF2F
benchmark \cite{minif2f}, which consists of 244 competition mathematics problems
formalized in Lean 4, using reinforcement learning from proof feedback and
Monte Carlo Tree Search with thousands of samples per problem.

This project asks a more constrained question: how much of that performance
can be recovered with a single GPU node, 4-bit quantized inference, and
roughly 400 samples per problem?
The headline answer is 48.8\%.
But the more useful answer lies in understanding \emph{why} the remaining
gap persists despite everything we tried, and what each failed attempt
reveals about the structure of the problem.

The project proceeded through three broad phases, each with a natural
failure mode.
\emph{Phase 1: scaling whole-proof sampling.}
Simply generating more independent proof attempts is the obvious first move.
Doubling the sample budget from 32 to 64 yields a large gain (+15.2 pp),
but quadrupling it again to 256 yields nothing: all 17 proofs the unverified
result claimed beyond 64 samples turned out to be verifier false positives.
The model's sampling distribution appears to ``max out'' on the set of problems
it can reach by the 64th attempt.
\emph{Phase 2: BFS tactic search.}
Adding BFS-guided tactic-level search partially breaks the plateau, but
the contribution is smaller than it appears.
Of the 25 problems the tactic search configuration uniquely solves, 8 are
genuinely attributable to the tree structure itself---short proofs
(average 1.9 tactics) that whole-proof sampling never generates.
The other 17 come from the whole-proof fallback having 400 samples
instead of 256, not from tree search at all.
Meanwhile 3 problems solved by $k=256$ are not solved by tactic search,
so the net gain is $25 - 3 = 22$.
\emph{Phase 3: fine-tuning.}
Two rounds of LoRA fine-tuning, with progressively more training data and
better hardware, both made things worse.
The problem was a mismatch: the best available Lean 4 training corpus
consists of 98\% single-line proofs, but the problems that needed help on
miniF2F require multi-step structured reasoning.

Each of these failures is, in its own way, informative.
The sampling plateau shows that the gap to 60.2\% is not a compute-scaling
problem within the whole-proof paradigm.
The partial success of tree search shows that structured search does help,
but only for a specific slice of problems---those where the correct first
tactic is discoverable by BFS within 6 steps.
The fine-tuning failure shows that the bottleneck is the training
distribution, not the model capacity.

\section{Background}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{The miniF2F Benchmark}

miniF2F \cite{minif2f} is a curated collection of 488 competition mathematics
problems (244 validation, 244 test) drawn from AMC, AIME, and IMO competitions,
formalized in Lean 4.
Each problem provides a complete Lean 4 theorem declaration ending in
\texttt{:= sorry}, and the task is to replace \texttt{sorry} with a valid proof.

The problems span three main categories: algebra (88 problems, 36\% of the
test set), number theory (68 problems, 28\%), and competition-style problems
from AMC/AIME/IMO (80 problems, 33\%), with the remaining 8 problems being
induction exercises.
Difficulty varies widely within each category: some algebra problems admit
one-line \texttt{ring} or \texttt{norm\_num} proofs, while others require
multi-step inequality arguments using specific Mathlib lemmas.
The 80 competition problems are uniformly hard: they require either the precise
right Mathlib API call or a structured multi-step argument that the model must
generate without any intermediate feedback.

\subsection{DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL}

DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL \cite{deepseek} is a 7-billion-parameter causal language
model derived from DeepSeek-Coder-V1.5-Instruct and further trained via reinforcement
learning on Lean 4 proof feedback.
Its inference protocol is \emph{whole-proof generation}: the model is given a
Lean 4 file stub ending with \texttt{:= by}, and generates the complete proof body
in a single forward pass, with no intermediate feedback.
The published result of 60.2\% on miniF2F-test uses roughly 3200 independent
samples per problem and a tree-search variant that applies at each step.

We use the publicly released weights under 4-bit NF4 quantization via
BitsAndBytes \cite{bitsandbytes}, reducing peak GPU memory from approximately
14 GB to 4 GB and enabling the full model to run on a single A40 GPU alongside
the Lean verification infrastructure.

\subsection{Lean 4 Verification}

Each candidate proof is verified by compiling it against the Mathlib library
using \texttt{lake build} \cite{mathlib}.
A proof is accepted if and only if the build exits with code 0 and no
\texttt{uses 'sorry'} warning appears in the output.
For tree-search verification, all candidates at each depth are compiled in a
single batch file, with errors attributed to individual theorems by tracking
line ranges in the shared file.
For whole-proof verification, up to 4 candidate files are compiled in parallel
to avoid exhausting GPU-node memory; each Lean elaboration process loads the
full Mathlib olean cache, approximately 8--12 GB per process.

Getting verification right turned out to be the hardest engineering challenge
of this project.
The batch verifier had two subtle bugs that caused it to report completions
when proofs were still incomplete:
(1) Lean sometimes reports ``unsolved goals'' at the blank separator line
after the last tactic, one line past the tracked range---fixing this
by extending each theorem's range by one line eliminated 51 false positives;
(2) when the model regenerates the theorem header as a ``tactic,'' Lean
silently closes the \texttt{by} block early, making the remaining goals
invisible to the line-range checker---adding a filter for candidates
matching \verb|^\s*theorem\s+\S| eliminated 17 additional false positives.
Together these bugs inflated one intermediate MCTS result from a true 30.7\%
to a claimed 58.6\%.
All final results reported here are verified by the corrected implementation.

\section{Methods}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Whole-Proof Sampling}

The baseline strategy generates $k$ independent proof attempts per problem via
temperature sampling ($T = 1.0$, $p = 0.95$), compiles them against Lean 4 via
\texttt{lake build}, and reports success if any candidate passes.
We evaluate at $k \in \{32, 64, 256\}$, with a 300-second timeout per
verification call and a per-proof token budget of 256 new tokens.

All generation runs use batch size 16, which we found to be the largest
stable batch size under 4-bit quantization.
Batch size 8 produces deadlocks in \texttt{torch.multinomial} on CUDA 12.2 due
to a known interaction with BitsAndBytes dequantization.

\subsection{BFS Tactic Search}
\label{sec:method_mcts}

The tactic search variant explores proof steps using breadth-first search
to depth at most 6, with width 8 (eight tactic candidates generated per node).
We note that this algorithm is commonly called ``MCTS'' in the theorem-proving
literature, including by the DeepSeek-Prover paper we compare against; however,
our implementation uses plain BFS with no UCB selection and no Monte Carlo rollouts.
We follow the field's loose convention in the tables and figures, but use
\emph{tactic search} or \emph{BFS search} in the body text.

At each node, the model's prompt includes the formal theorem statement and
the sequence of tactics applied so far; it is asked to generate the next
single tactic.
At each depth, all $8 \times |\text{frontier}|$ tactic candidates are compiled
in a single \texttt{lake build} call against a shared \texttt{ProofGoals.lean}
file.
Candidates are classified as: (a) complete---no errors; (b) valid partial---only
``unsolved goals'' errors remain; (c) invalid---any other error.
Only valid partials are retained for the next depth.

After the search exhausts its 120-second budget without finding a proof,
the problem enters a whole-proof fallback phase: the model generates up to 400
independent whole-proof candidates via the standard generation procedure.
This combined budget of tactic search followed by fallback constitutes the
BFS configuration reported in \autoref{tab:main_results}.

\subsection{LoRA Fine-Tuning}

We conducted two rounds of LoRA fine-tuning on the DeepSeek-Prover base model,
both using whole-proof training pairs (Lean 4 file stub, complete proof body)
to match the inference format.

\textbf{Version 1} trained on 67 examples: validated miniF2F proofs found
by the base model on the validation split.
This run used 4-bit NF4 quantization with float16 compute and LoRA rank 16.

\textbf{Version 2} addressed data scarcity by adding 10,434 proofs from the
Lean-Workbook dataset \cite{leanworkbook} (filtered to examples with
\texttt{state\_after = "no goals"}), giving 10,561 total training examples.
This run used full BF16 precision and LoRA rank 64 on an A100-class GPU.

\section{Analysis}

\subsection{Why Whole-Proof Sampling Saturates at 39.8\%}
\label{sec:scaling}

\autoref{tab:scaling} shows the marginal gain from increasing the whole-proof
sample budget.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Marginal gains from increasing the sample budget. All counts are
verified (false positives excluded). ``New'' counts problems not proved at
the previous $k$.}
\label{tab:scaling}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccr@{}}
\toprule
Run & Proved & Pass@1 & New verified proofs \\
\midrule
$k = 32$ & 60 & 24.6\% & -- \\
$k = 64$ & 97 & 39.8\% & $+$37 \\
$k = 256$ & 97 & 39.8\% & $\phantom{+}$0 \\
MCTS ($k \approx 400$ total) & 119 & 48.8\% & $+$22 vs.\ verified $k=256$ \\
\midrule
Published ($k \approx 3200$) & 147 & 60.2\% & -- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The pattern is striking.
Doubling from 32 to 64 samples gains 37 theorems---a large, clean jump.
Quadrupling to 256 gains exactly zero.
The 17 proofs that the unverified $k=256$ result claimed beyond $k=64$
were all batch-verifier false positives; after re-verification with
\texttt{lake env lean}, the score is identical to $k=64$.

Understanding \emph{why} this plateau occurs requires examining what the 37
new proofs from $k=32 \to 64$ actually look like.
At $k=32$, the model proves problems whose correct proof appears in the top
few samples: mostly one- or two-tactic closers (\texttt{norm\_num},
\texttt{omega}, \texttt{ring}, short arithmetic chains).
The 37 problems gained at $k=64$ are the next tier: they still have
``easy'' proofs by Lean standards, but the model only generates the correct
tactic sequence reliably one in 40--60 tries.
Once both of these groups are exhausted, the remaining 147 problems are
structurally different: their proofs require either (a) a rare, highly
specific Mathlib API call that the model generates with very low probability,
or (b) a multi-step argument where each step depends on the previous.
For such problems, drawing more independent full-proof samples provides no
additional coverage---every draw is effectively a fresh gamble with very
low probability of success.

Proof-length statistics confirm this picture.
Among the 97 problems solved by whole-proof sampling at $k=256$, 75\%
(73/97) require multi-step proofs.
These were accessible to sampling because the model has seen and learned
their proof patterns during RL training.
The 147 unsolved problems require proofs that the model's distribution
simply does not cover---adding samples only reruns the same low-probability
trials.

\subsection{What Tree Search Adds---and What It Does Not}
\label{sec:analysis_mcts}

\autoref{tab:overlap} compares MCTS and $k=256$ sampling problem-by-problem.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering

\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}

Only MCTS proved & 25 & 10.2\% & 10.9 \\
\quad --- found by tree search & (8) & (3.3\%) & 1.9 \\
\quad --- found by whole-proof fallback & (17) & (7.0\%) & 15.2 \\

The 25 MCTS-unique problems decompose into two distinct groups with very
different explanations.

\textbf{Eight problems found by tree search} (average 1.9 tactics, median 1).
These are short proofs---many are single-tactic solutions like
\texttt{nlinarith} applied after a \texttt{subst}---where the correct
tactic was either absent from the whole-proof model's distribution or
consistently garbled in its full-proof context.
Tree search finds them by exploring the space of first tactics
systematically: even if the model assigns the correct tactic low probability
in a full-proof context, it surfaces that tactic at depth 1 within 8 attempts.

\textbf{Seventeen problems found by the whole-proof fallback} (average 15.2 tactics, median 16).
These are long, structured proofs: \texttt{have} chains, intermediate
lemma applications, calculation blocks.
They were \emph{not} found by tree search and were not found by the
separate $k=256$ run---but the MCTS configuration's fallback uses 400
samples rather than 256.
The most natural explanation is simply more samples: these are problems
that required a lucky draw from the model's distribution, and 400
attempts exceeded the threshold while 256 did not.

This decomposition reveals a confound in interpreting the MCTS result.
The ``9 pp improvement from MCTS'' conflates two contributions:
roughly 3.3 pp from tree search structure itself, and roughly 7 pp from
the fallback's larger sample budget.
To cleanly isolate the tree search contribution, one would need a
matched $k=400$ whole-proof baseline, which we did not run.

The 3 problems solved only by $k=256$ but not MCTS are also instructive.
All three are proofs that whole-proof sampling found by luck in 256 attempts,
but whose first tactic---the one that opens the correct proof path---
has very low probability in the model's distribution.
At tree-search depth 1, 8 tactic candidates were generated per problem;
these correct first tactics were not among them.
This is a fundamental limitation of BFS with fixed width: if the correct
proof path starts with a low-probability tactic, the tree never finds it.

Several features of this table are worth examining in detail.

\textbf{Algebra saturates quickly.}
The pass rate jumps from 25\% at $k=32$ to 58\% at $k=64$---the largest
single-step gain of any category---then barely moves to 59\% at $k=256$
and 66\% with MCTS.
Algebra problems that the model can solve tend to have short, mechanically
derivable proofs (\texttt{linarith}, \texttt{ring}, \texttt{nlinarith}),
and the model finds these quickly.
The remaining 30 unsolved algebra problems presumably require structured
arguments that exceed what either strategy can construct.

\textbf{Number theory shows anomalous behavior at $k=64$.}
The pass rate \emph{drops} from 47\% to 43\% between $k=32$ and $k=64$.
This is not a true regression but a stochastic artifact: the two runs
were independent (not seeded from each other), and the set of problems
proved changes with each random sampling.
The verified $k=32$ run found 32 number theory proofs; the independent
$k=64$ run found only 29---a different mix, missing some problems $k=32$
solved while finding others in their place.
This illustrates that pass@1 estimates carry sampling variance at the
problem level, especially for categories where success probability is
close to the threshold.
By MCTS the count recovers to 32---matching $k=32$'s count but on a
somewhat different set of problems.

\textbf{Competition problems are where MCTS earns most of its keep.}
At $k=256$, only 13/80 competition problems are solved (16\%).
MCTS doubles this to 26/80 (33\%).
These 13 additional competition proofs are the most meaningful output of
the tree search: they are problems where the correct proof requires
exactly the right sequence of 2--5 tactics, and tree search finds this
sequence even though no 256-sample whole-proof attempt ever produced it.

Nevertheless, 67\% of competition problems (54/80) remain unsolved by
any configuration.
These 54 problems are the core of the gap to 60.2\%.
Their proofs require either long structured arguments ($>6$ tactics)
that exceed BFS depth, or uncommon Mathlib lemmas that the model's
distribution assigns very low probability to.

\subsection{Fine-Tuning Degrades Whole-Proof Generation}
\label{sec:lora}

Both LoRA fine-tuning attempts degraded performance relative to the
unmodified base model, and the degradation \emph{increased} with more data:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{LoRA fine-tuning results compared to base model, all at $k=32$.}
\label{tab:lora}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llccc@{}}
\toprule
Model & Training data & Proved & Pass@1 & $\Delta$ vs.\ base \\
\midrule
DeepSeek base & -- & 60 & 24.6\% & -- \\
DeepSeek + LoRA V1 & 67 miniF2F proofs & 45 & 18.4\% & $-$6.2 pp \\
DeepSeek + LoRA V2 & 10,561 proofs & 41 & 16.8\% & $-$7.8 pp \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

LoRA V1 used 67 training examples (validated miniF2F proofs from the
validation split) with 4-bit quantization and LoRA rank 16.
LoRA V2 expanded to 10,561 training examples by adding 10,434 Lean-Workbook
proofs and used full BF16 precision with LoRA rank 64 on an A100-class GPU.
The extra data and better hardware both made things worse.

The root cause is a distributional mismatch that is visible in the training
corpus itself.
When we examine the Lean-Workbook proofs used for LoRA V2, we find that
the length distribution is almost entirely degenerate:

\begin{itemize}
\item 98.0\% of training proofs are single-line (one tactic closes the goal)
\item 0.8\% require exactly two lines
\item 1.2\% require three or more lines
\end{itemize}

This distribution reflects the nature of the Lean-Workbook collection: it
consists of standalone lemmas, many of which are trivial verifications or
one-step consequences of standard theorems.
The base model, trained via reinforcement learning on the full difficulty
range of Lean 4 formal mathematics, had learned to generate structured
multi-step proofs for problems that require them.
After fine-tuning on a corpus that is 98\% single-step closers, the model's
output distribution shifts strongly toward generating a single tactic and
stopping---the correct behavior for 98\% of what it was trained on, but
precisely wrong for miniF2F.

The magnitude of this effect is visible in the proof output.
All 41 proofs found by LoRA V2 use one of five single-step closers:
\texttt{omega}, \texttt{linarith}, \texttt{ring}, \texttt{norm\_num}, or \texttt{rfl}.
This is \emph{identical} to what the unmodified base model finds greedily
at $k=1$---the fine-tuned model has effectively regressed to greedy decoding.
The 15 problems that LoRA V2 fails to prove but the base model solves
at $k=32$ are all multi-step proofs; the fine-tuned model generates the
wrong (truncated) proof for each of them.

Among the 119 problems solved by the best MCTS configuration, 75 (63\%)
require proofs with more than one tactic step.
Fine-tuning suppressed exactly this capability.
The mismatch between training distribution (98\% single-step) and target
distribution (63\% multi-step) explains the degradation cleanly.

\begin{obs}
Fine-tuning a whole-proof generation model on single-step training data
is counterproductive for benchmarks that require multi-step reasoning.
Degradation worsens with more single-step training data, because more data
amplifies the distributional bias.
Effective fine-tuning requires training pairs whose proof-length and
difficulty distributions match the target benchmark.
\end{obs}

\subsection{Why 48.8\% and Not 60\%}
\label{sec:gap}

The published 60.2\% result \cite{deepseek} differs from ours along several
dimensions that are worth separating.

\textbf{Sample count is necessary but not sufficient.}
The paper uses approximately 3200 samples per problem; we use roughly 400
across tree search and fallback.
Naively, one might expect this 8$\times$ gap to explain the performance
difference.
But our scaling analysis in \autoref{sec:scaling} shows that the whole-proof
regime saturates by $k=64$.
Even 3200 independent whole-proof samples would not close the gap if the
model's sampling distribution simply does not cover the hard problems.
The key difference is that the published system integrates tree search
\emph{throughout} its sample budget---it generates new tactic candidates at
each BFS node rather than spending the entire budget on independent
full-proof attempts.
This lets it make productive use of 3200 samples in a way that our
sequential (tree search then fallback) approach does not.

\textbf{The hard tail is structural.}
Of the 244 test problems, 122 (50\%) are solved by neither whole-proof
sampling nor our MCTS configuration.
These problems are concentrated in the competition category (54/80 unsolved).
Inspection of their structures suggests two failure modes: proofs longer
than our BFS depth limit of 6 tactics, and proofs that require highly
specific Mathlib API calls (\texttt{Nat.Coprime}, \texttt{Int.emod\_emod\_of\_dvd},
\texttt{Finset.sum\_congr}, etc.) that the model's distribution assigns very
low probability.
The published system solves $147 - 119 = 28$ of these 122 problems.
This is plausibly explained by the deeper effective search (via integrated tree
search with 3200 samples) combined with the full-precision model having a
slightly better-calibrated distribution over rare Mathlib lemmas.

\textbf{Quantization effects.}
We run 4-bit NF4 quantized inference, while the published results use
full precision.
4-bit quantization reduces memory by $\sim$3.5$\times$ but introduces
quantization noise that may slightly perturb the model's distribution
over rare tokens---precisely the Mathlib API calls that hard problems require.
We did not measure this effect in isolation.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{The ByT5 Tactic Model}
\label{sec:byt5}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

As a secondary experiment, we evaluated ByT5-small \cite{byt5}
(300M parameters, byte-level tokenizer), fine-tuned on Mathlib tactic data
from LeanDojo \cite{leandojo}.
ByT5 generates one tactic at a time from a proof state, rather than a complete proof.
On miniF2F, it is augmented with 14 universal fallback tactics
(\texttt{norm\_num}, \texttt{omega}, \texttt{ring}, \texttt{linarith}, \texttt{decide},
\texttt{aesop}, and combinations).

After fine-tuning on the full Mathlib tactic dataset for 5 epochs
(${\approx}250\text{K}$ training examples), ByT5 reaches 70/244 = 28.7\%, surpassing
the published ReProver result of ${\approx}26.5\%$.
However, comparing the pretrained and fine-tuned variants suggests this
performance is almost entirely explained by the 14 fallback tactics, which
mechanically close the straightforward miniF2F problems: the pretrained ByT5
already achieves 24.2\%, with fine-tuning on the full Mathlib tactic corpus
adding only 4.5 pp (28.7\% vs.\ 24.2\%);
this suggests ByT5 contributes most usefully as a tactic-ranking mechanism
that occasionally selects the right close from the fallback set, rather than
as a proof-search engine.

More fundamentally, ByT5 and DeepSeek probe different capabilities.
ByT5 is a 300M-parameter tactic predictor that generates one step at a time
from a proof state, requiring an interactive REPL to elaborate.
DeepSeek is a 7B-parameter causal model that generates entire multi-step
proofs in one shot.
The two approaches should be complementary: ByT5 provides precise tactic
guidance at each state, while DeepSeek provides long-range planning across
the full proof.
Integrating them---for instance, using ByT5 to rank tree-search candidates
at each BFS node---is a natural direction for future work.

\section{Implementation Challenges}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

Several engineering issues complicated the evaluation in ways that are worth
documenting for reproducibility.

\textbf{Verifier false positives.}
This was the most consequential implementation problem.
Three distinct MCTS experiments produced three different apparent scores
before verification errors were fully resolved:
(a) an initial regex bug caused the batch verifier to never match any
error line, so every first-tactic candidate appeared to succeed---inflating
the result from $\approx$4\% to 99.6\%;
(b) after fixing the regex, two subtler bugs in line-range attribution
still inflated the score from the true 30.7\% to a claimed 58.6\%;
(c) after fixing both (the range extension and the theorem-header filter),
the clean run produced the verified 48.8\%.
The lesson is that in-process batch verification of Lean proofs is
substantially harder to get right than it appears, and any result that
looks surprisingly good should be re-verified by an independent method
before being reported.

\textbf{Thread limits on CPU nodes.}
Lean spawns threads for parallel tactic elaboration.
CPU cluster nodes impose strict thread limits that caused SIGABRT
when reverification was run on them.
All reverification was moved to GPU nodes, and
\texttt{LEAN\_NUM\_THREADS=1} was added to the subprocess environment
as a belt-and-suspenders fix.

\textbf{Memory constraints.}
Running 8 parallel \texttt{lake env lean} processes, each loading the
full Mathlib olean cache (${\approx}8$--12 GB per process), exhausted
100 GB nodes.
The whole-proof verification pipeline was capped at 4 parallel workers.

\textbf{BPE decode artifacts.}
DeepSeek's tokenizer produces Unicode byte-pair symbols (\texttt{Ġ},
\texttt{Ċ}) that must be post-processed to spaces and newlines.
Without this, all generated proofs are garbled and the model appears to
achieve 0\%; discovering the root cause of this failure cost several hours.

\textbf{CUDA deadlock at batch size 8.}
A known bug in BitsAndBytes dequantization on CUDA 12.2 causes
\texttt{torch.multinomial} to deadlock at batch size 8.
Only batch size 16 (larger, counterintuitively) was stable.

\textbf{Specific problems that cause silent hangs.}
Four miniF2F problems (\texttt{numbertheory\_4x3m7y3neq2003},
\texttt{numbertheory\_x5neqy2p4}, \texttt{amc12\_2000\_p6},
\texttt{mathd\_numbertheory\_66}) caused \texttt{model.generate} to
deadlock silently under 4-bit quantization.
These are problems whose formal statements contain the $\neq$ operator
formalized in a way that produces unusual token sequences.
The BF16 model does not hang on these problems.
We skip these four in generation; they are counted as failures in the
reported 244-problem totals.

\section{Conclusion}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

We evaluated DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL on miniF2F under constrained compute,
reaching 48.8\% with a tree search and fallback configuration---9 percentage
points above the whole-proof sampling ceiling and 24 pp above the simplest
baseline.
The path there was not straightforward, and the obstacles are the
substance of the result.

The main findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Whole-proof sampling saturates by $k=64$.}
From 32 to 64 samples, the verified score jumps by 15 pp.
From 64 to 256, it gains nothing.
This plateau is real: all 17 proofs the unverified $k=256$ result claimed
beyond $k=64$ were false positives.
The model's sampling distribution does not cover the harder half of the
benchmark regardless of how many samples are drawn.

\item \textbf{Tree search helps, but less than the headline suggests.}
Of the 25 problems uniquely solved by MCTS (but not by $k=256$), 8 are
genuinely attributable to tree structure (short proofs found at BFS depth 1--2),
and 17 come from the larger whole-proof sample budget in the fallback phase
(400 vs.\ 256 samples).
Since 3 problems solved by $k=256$ are not solved by MCTS, the net gain is
$25 - 3 = 22$.
Pure tree search contributes $\approx$3 pp; the remaining $\approx$6 pp is
additional sampling.

\item \textbf{Fine-tuning on the wrong distribution actively hurts.}
When 98\% of training proofs are single-step closers but the target
benchmark requires multi-step reasoning, fine-tuning suppresses exactly
the capability that is needed.
Degradation worsens with more data, because more data deepens the bias.
This pattern---``more data, more damage''---should be a warning sign for
any fine-tuning experiment where the training and target difficulty
distributions are not carefully matched.

\item \textbf{The gap to 60\% is structural, not computational.}
Closing from 48.8\% to 60.2\% requires solving 28 additional problems,
concentrated in the competition category.
These problems require proof structures---long tactic chains ($>6$ steps),
uncommon Mathlib API calls---that neither more whole-proof samples nor
6-step BFS can produce.
The path forward involves either a deeper/wider search strategy that
integrates sampling across all tree nodes (as the published system does),
or a fine-tuning corpus that genuinely covers the difficulty range of
competition mathematics.
\end{enumerate}
